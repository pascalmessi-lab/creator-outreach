# Pitches and follow-ups

## Who reads the pitch

Usually a partnerships, influencer or brand marketing manager who gets dozens of creator emails a week, most of them generic. They're checking three things fast: *Is this person's work good and on-brand? Is the idea something I could get approved? Is it easy to say yes to a next step?* Write for that reader.

What gets ignored: "I love your brand", follower counts with no context, "let me know if you'd like to collaborate", attaching a media kit instead of an idea, and anything that could be sent to any brand by changing the name.

## The five parts of a good pitch

1. **Who** — sender and creator in one sentence, with the creator's niche in plain words.
2. **Why this brand** — one specific, supported detail: a product, a launch, a shop, a past creator campaign, a gap in their content. Specific beats flattering.
3. **The idea** — a named, concrete deliverable they can picture: format, length, platform, and the moment the product shows up. "A 45-second TikTok where Nia sketches a Manchester street scene start to finish in a Paper North A5 sketchbook" beats "content featuring your sketchbooks".
4. **Why now** — a reason to act this month: their launch or season, a relevant date, the creator's upcoming project or exhibition. Use a real one or leave it out; never invent urgency.
5. **One easy ask** — a yes/no question that costs nothing: "Shall I send a one-page outline?" or "Are you the right person, or is there a partnerships contact?"

Then one proof point if there is a true one (a comparable past collaboration, a post that performed well with its supplied figure and date, an exhibition, a press mention), and the exact supplied links.

## Making the idea land

- **Show the product doing something.** The best concepts make the product part of the process (the tool, the canvas, the setting, the before/after), not a prop at the end.
- **Match the format to proven work.** Propose what the creator already makes well, so the brand can see a past example of the same format.
- **Offer a small, bounded first step.** A single video or one artwork is easier to approve than a "long-term ambassadorship". Larger packages can follow.
- **Give the brand a use.** Say where the content could live: the creator's channels, the brand's social, a shop window, a product page. This is a proposal, and usage rights are agreed later.
- **No metrics? Lead with the work.** A clear idea plus two links to great examples beats weak numbers. If numbers are supplied and strong, put one in context ("her process videos average 40k views on TikTok, Sept–Oct 2026, from her analytics").

## Subject lines

Name the creator and the idea or format, not the relationship. Keep it under about 60 characters so it isn't cut off.

- Good: `Sketch-to-print video idea for Paper North`
- Good: `Nia Vale × Paper North: Manchester street-scene series`
- Weak: `Collaboration opportunity`, `Partnership inquiry`, `Love your brand!`

## Template

> Subject A: [Specific idea] for [Brand]
> Subject B: [Creator] × [Brand]: [format]
>
> Hi [confirmed name, or Brand team],
>
> I'm [Sender], [Creator]'s manager. [Creator] [one true sentence about the work, in plain words].
>
> [Supported brand detail] gave me an idea: [concrete deliverable with format, platform and where the product appears]. [Why now, if real.]
>
> [Optional proof point.] Her work: [label]: [exact link] · [label]: [exact link]
>
> [One yes/no ask]
>
> [Sender]

Replace every placeholder before calling a draft ready. Never invent a named recipient to fill the greeting.

## Worked example (fictional)

> Subject A: Sketch-to-print video idea for Paper North
> Subject B: Nia Vale × Paper North: Manchester street scenes
>
> Hi Paper North team,
>
> I'm Alex, manager of Nia Vale, a Manchester illustrator whose sketchbook process videos show a drawing from first line to finished print.
>
> Seeing your new A5 sketchbook range alongside the Northern Quarter shop gave me an idea for a paid video: Nia draws one Manchester street (say, Oldham Street) start to finish in a Paper North sketchbook, ending on the finished print. It would suit your autumn range on TikTok and Instagram, and the original could be displayed in the shop.
>
> You can see the same format here: Instagram: https://instagram.example/niavale · TikTok: https://tiktok.example/@niavale
>
> Shall I send a one-page outline with deliverables?
>
> Alex Reed

Why it works: the product is part of the process, the idea is visual and approvable as one video, there is a local hook and a seasonal reason, the use is suggested but not promised, and the ask takes ten seconds to answer. (The "new range" and shop detail must come from a real source in actual use.)

## Short DM

40–70 words. Who, one specific idea, and a request for the right business contact. Don't paste the email, don't DM several staff, and don't imply a relationship that doesn't exist.

## Follow-ups

Each follow-up must add something: a quick sketch or mock-up of the concept, a new relevant post, or a timely hook. "Just bumping this" adds nothing.

- **Follow-up 1** (~5 business days after send): one or two lines plus the new item. Reply in the same thread.
- **Follow-up 2** (~12 business days): a polite close: "I'll assume the timing isn't right; happy to share future ideas if useful."

Never invent results or deadlines to justify a follow-up. An out-of-office reply means picking a date after their return, not following up automatically.

## Handling replies

Sort the reply first: interested, wants more information, gifted or affiliate offer, budget question, negotiation, redirect to someone else, or decline.

**Gifted offer on a paid brief.** Thank them, keep the door open, restate the paid goal and offer a smaller paid option:

> Thanks so much — Nia would love to work with Paper North. For sponsored content she works on a paid basis, but we can keep it light: a single process video rather than a series. Shall I send a simple outline with costs?

Only offer gifted work if the user has said it's acceptable (for example, for a brand the creator especially wants on their portfolio).

**"What are your rates?"** Use the user's rate card or minimum. If there isn't one, don't make up a price. Ask the user, and reply asking about their budget and scope ("Could you share the budget range and usage you have in mind? Then I can put together options").

**Low offer.** Before countering, adjust the scope rather than dropping the price: fewer deliverables, organic-only usage, a shorter usage period, no exclusivity.

## Terms to pin down before agreeing anything

These are questions to settle, not things the brand gets by default:

- **Deliverables:** number, format, length, revision rounds, approval process.
- **Posting:** platforms, dates, whether the brand must approve before posting.
- **Usage:** organic reposting versus paid ads. Running ads through the creator's account (Instagram partnership ads, TikTok Spark Ads, often called "whitelisting") is a separate, paid permission with a duration.
- **Usage period and territory**, and **exclusivity** (which competitors, for how long). Both should raise the price.
- **Costs and payment:** materials, travel, payment terms (e.g. 30 days), and deposit for commissioned work.

Don't give legal conclusions; flag terms for the user, or their adviser, to review.

## Disclosure (UK)

Paid or gifted content usually has to be clearly labelled as advertising (for example "Ad" at the start of the caption) under UK advertising rules and consumer law. ASA and CMA guidance covers this. When proposing deliverables, assume they'll be labelled, and don't suggest content that hides the commercial relationship. For other markets, note that local rules apply and suggest the user checks them. Don't state specific legal requirements as certain; point to current official guidance.
